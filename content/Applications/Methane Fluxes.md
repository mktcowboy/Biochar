# Methane Fluxes

How soil-applied biochar changes **methane (CH₄)** exchange in agricultural systems—especially the contrast between **flooded paddies** and **upland / non-flooded** soils. Hub: [[GHG Fluxes]]. Sisters: [[Nitrous Oxide Fluxes]], [[Priming and SOC Interactions]].

Report context: **2026-09-16**.

---

## 1. Why CH₄ is messy

Soils can be CH₄ **sources** (methanogenesis in anaerobic microsites / paddies) or **sinks** (methanotrophy in oxic soils). A positive Hedge’s *d* can mean either a weaker sink or a stronger source—Jeffery et al. (2016) spell out that ambiguity explicitly ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.soilbio.2016.07.021); author manuscript PDF on file under `/workspace/biochar-research/jeffery-2016-ch4-meta.pdf`).

Global agricultural CH₄ is net positive because rice cultivation sources dwarf upland sinks—so paddy mitigation can matter even if upland sink strength sometimes drops (Jeffery 2016 abstract conclusion).

---

## 2. Jeffery et al. 2016 meta-analysis (core)

Jeffery, Verheijen, Kammann & Abalos (2016), *Soil Biology & Biochemistry* 101:251–258 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.soilbio.2016.07.021)):

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Screened / included | 62 studies screened → **42** included; **189** pairwise comparisons |
| Metric | Hedge’s *d* (standardised mean difference) |
| **Flooded** (management includes flooding) | *d* = **−0.87** — increased sink / reduced source strength |
| **Non-flooded** | *d* = **+0.65** — decreased sink / increased source strength |
| **Acidic soils (pH < 6)** | *d* = **−1.56** — strongest mitigation direction |
| **Neutral** | *d* = **+1.17** |
| **Alkaline** | *d* = **+0.44** |

**Practice reading:** biochar is most promising for CH₄ in **flooded / paddy / acidic** systems; in **non-flooded neutral–alkaline** uplands it may weaken the CH₄ sink. Do not paste a single global % onto every farm.

## 3. Jia et al. 2023 global mean

Jia et al. (2023): mean **CH₄ emissions −13.0%**; CH₄ **uptake unchanged** in that analysis; pools incubation, pot, and field ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ldr.4868) · [[Global GHG and Yield Patterns|global GHG and yield patterns]]). GHG intensity **−14.3%** overall (−18.7% upland, −14.5% paddy) in the same paper. Field responses were weaker than lab/pot—treat headline % cautiously.

---

## 4. Inventory default (Woolf 2021)

Woolf et al. (2021) assume **no net CH₄ change** for national inventory–style accounting—too few field data to predict size or direction reliably ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c02425) · [[Biochar Soil GHG Inventory Model|models soil GHG from biochar]]). Farm NPV and credit calculators should not invent a CH₄ co-benefit unless the methodology and site data support it.

## 5. Composting-process CH₄ (different boundary)

During **composting**, biochar often cuts CH₄ strongly (anaerobic pockets / methanogen–methanotroph balance):

| Source | CH₄ effect | URL |
|---|---|---|
| Li et al. 2025 composting meta | Mean **−53.7%** | https://doi.org/10.48130/nc-0025-0003 |
| Maturation meta 2025 | **−51.31%** | https://doi.org/10.48130/bchax-0025-0005 |
| Pacific white paper (cited studies) | Some studies **>80%** lower | https://pacificbiochar.com/wp-content/uploads/Pacific-Biochar_Biochar-Compost_white-paper.pdf |
| Yin et al. 2021 review | Mitigation possible; ~10% w/w often discussed; granular form favors CH₄ cuts | https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2021.149294 |

These are **facility** emissions—not soil CH₄ after land application ([[Co-composting Science]], [[Matrix Pathways for Credits]]).

## 7. Mechanisms (qualitative, from the CH₄ meta discussion)

Jeffery et al. (2016) synthesize literature mechanisms without claiming a single pathway dominates ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.soilbio.2016.07.021)):

- Improved aeration / redox shifts in flooded soils may favor methanotrophs or suppress methanogens.
- pH rises from alkaline chars can alter methanogen/methanotroph balance (acidic soils showed the strongest sink-strengthening *d*).
- Labile C additions can temporarily fuel methanogenesis—another reason to prefer matured co-composted products when CH₄ is a concern ([[Co-composting Science]]).
- In oxic uplands, biochar effects on the CH₄ **sink** may reverse the flooded-system story (positive *d* in non-flooded classes).

Pair with N₂O mechanisms ([[Nitrous Oxide Fluxes]]) and priming ([[Priming and SOC Interactions]]) when building a full GHG narrative.

---

## 8. Farm decision rules

1. **Paddy / flooded acidic soils:** CH₄ co-benefit is plausible per Jeffery 2016—still measure if claiming.
2. **Temperate upland neutral soils:** do not assume CH₄ mitigation; sink weakening is possible.
3. **Inventory / credit defaults:** follow Woolf **no net CH₄** unless the methodology says otherwise ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c02425)).
4. **Compost facilities:** process CH₄ cuts can be large—account under composting MRV, not soil CORCs ([[Matrix Pathways for Credits]]).
