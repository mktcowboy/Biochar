# Gasification Residues

Chapter on **solids from biomass gasification**—often high-ash, lower organic-carbon materials that may still qualify as biochar when organic C meets definitional and permanence criteria. Report context: **2026-09-16**.

**Related notes:** [[Production]] · [[Woody vs Manure Ag Feedstocks]] · [[Biochar Quality from Process]] · [[Heavy Metals and Ash]] · [[Woolf IPCC Fperm]] · [[Production Hub]] · [[Key Sources]]

---

## 1. What gasification leaves behind

Gasification converts biomass with a **limited oxidant** supply to prioritize **syngas** (CO, H₂, CO₂, light hydrocarbons). The solid residue retains ash plus whatever organic carbon did not gasify. Woolf notes these materials may be described as **“ash with biochar”** or **“high-ash content biochar”**—no universal naming standard ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c02425) §3.1).

IPCC App. 4 and Woolf **include** gasification solids in the biochar inventory method when they meet the >350 °C limited-oxidant definition ([IPCC PDF](https://www.ipcc-nggip.iges.or.jp/public/2019rf/pdf/4_Volume4/19R_V4_Ch02_Ap4_Biochar.pdf); [Woolf §2.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c02425)). EBC likewise allows gasification within its technology spectrum if quality and sustainability rules are met ([EBC 10.5E §2](https://www.european-biochar.org/media/doc/7/ebc_en_10_5_red.pdf)).

## 2. Carbon fractions (Woolf Table 2 gasification column)

| Feedstock | Gasification FC (mean) | Contrast: pyrolysis mean FC |
|---|---|---|
| Wood | **0.63** | 0.76 |
| Herbaceous | 0.38 | 0.64 |
| Manure | **0.14** | 0.39 |
| Sewage sludge | **0.10** | 0.37 |
| Rice residues | 0.20 | 0.47 |

([open PDF](https://open-science.canada.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/112fc297-0352-4683-ae0e-dd1271f59d77/content) Table 2). Abstract extremes: gasified biosolids C content as low as **~7%** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c02425)).

**If ash has been partially removed** from a gasification solid, Woolf Table 2 defaults **must not** be used—measure FC directly ([§3.1](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c02425)).

## 3. Permanence paradox (low FC, possibly high persistence)

Gasification typically runs hot → **low H/C_org** → organic C that remains can be highly condensed and score well on Woolf H/C_org permanence regressions ([§3.2](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c02425)). IPCC high-T class explicitly contemplates high-T gasification for \(F_\mathrm{perm}\) **0.89** ([PDF](https://www.ipcc-nggip.iges.or.jp/public/2019rf/pdf/4_Volume4/19R_V4_Ch02_Ap4_Biochar.pdf)).

**Accounting implication:** tonnes of **product** are ash-diluted; tonnes of **durable organic C** may still be valuable. Always multiply \(M \times F_C \times F_\mathrm{perm}\), never assume wood-like FC.

Hansen et al. (2015) discuss gasification biochar as a co-product for sequestration and soil amendment in the broader literature Woolf cites ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biombioe.2014.11.010)).

## 4. Agronomy and contaminants

- **Ash / salts / liming:** can supply K, Ca, Mg and raise pH—but salinity and heavy-metal concentration risks rise ([[Heavy Metals and Ash]]).
- **Nutrients:** manure/sludge gasification residues concentrate P/K relative to woody pyrochars but may fail EBC-Agro metal limits ([EBC §4.9](https://www.european-biochar.org/media/doc/7/ebc_en_10_5_red.pdf)).
- **PAHs:** still process-dependent; poor vapor handling can contaminate residues ([[PAHs]]).
- **Naming for buyers:** disclose FC, ash, H/C_org, and feedstock—avoid selling “biochar” without ash context.

---

## 5. Practice takeaway

1. Treat gasification solids as **first-class inventory materials** only with measured FC + H/C_org (or documented high-T class).
2. Expect **CDR per tonne product** ≪ woody slow-pyrolysis char.
3. Screen metals/PFAS before soil use—ash concentration amplifies feedstock contamination.
