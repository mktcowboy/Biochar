Soil and Agriculture

Chapter-length map of biochar in farm and soil systems: how it changes soil, what yield and GHG metas say, how to apply it, named real-world deployments, products/practices, constraints, and links to Permanence, NRCS soil carbon amendment in US ag, and carbon-market soil pathways.

Report context: 2026-09-16.

Purpose and scope

Most historical agronomy and a large share of voluntary biochar CDR still assume soil end-use. Outcomes are context-specific (texture, pH, climate, rate, feedstock/process, fertilizer regime). This note synthesizes primary metas, NRCS practice guidance, and named projects—not universal prescriptions.

1. Soil mechanisms

1.1 Water retention and physical structure

Meta-analyses generally show larger hydrological benefits in coarse-textured soils:

  • Omondi et al. (2016) quantified mean effects including available water capacity (AWC) +15.1%, porosity +8.4%, bulk density −7.6%, and saturated hydraulic conductivity +25.2% across compiled studies (DOI).
  • Razzaghi et al. (2020) systematic review/meta-analysis asks whether biochar improves water retention and finds texture- and rate-dependent gains (DOI).
  • Wei et al. (2023) (939 observations) report field capacity / AWC responses by texture: coarse FC +23.8% / AWC +25.6%; medium +5.0% / +20.9%; fine +7.2% / +11.5% (DOI).
  • Blanco-Canqui (2017) reviews physical-property pathways (bulk density, porosity, aggregation, hydraulics) as functions of rate, feedstock, and soil (DOI).

Practice implication: sandy / low-OM soils are the clearest hydrological use-case; fine clays may show smaller AWC gains for the same rate.

1.2 Nutrients, CEC, and liming / pH

meta-analyzes crop productivity interpreted stronger productivity responses in acidic (+14%) and coarse/medium soils as consistent with liming and water-holding mechanisms, plus improved nutrient availability (DOI). compares tropical vs temperate yields found mean tropical yield benefits (~+25%) versus little mean benefit in temperate systems, again consistent with acid / low-fertility soil chemistry (DOI).

USDA NRCS CPS 336 guidance (April 2026 FAQ) states biochar is usually alkaline and most useful as a liming agent on acid soils; on soils already pH > 7, check liming equivalency and add cautiously. The FAQ walks liming-offset arithmetic (e.g., 1 ton biochar/acre at 9% CaCO₃ liming equivalency offsets ~9% of a 1 ton lime recommendation) (NRCS FAQ PDF · answers CPS 336 planning questions).

NRCS also flags electrical conductivity (EC) / salts: high-ash manure chars can raise salinity; wood chars are typically lower EC. Spec-sheet examples in the FAQ contrast high-C wood char (low EC) vs poultry-manure char (high EC, high P/K, high liming) (same FAQ).

CEC and nutrient sorption are feedstock- and aging-dependent; reviews biochar climate mitigation summarize nutrient and liming co-benefits in the broader CDR/agronomy framing (DOI). Fresh, uninoculated biochar can temporarily immobilize nutrients—NRCS explicitly warns that “fresh biochar … has a chance to reduce crop yields by binding and immobilizing nutrients” and recommends compost/manure charging (FAQ; national standard also: inoculate with compost, compost tea, or manure — CPS 336 2022 PDF · defines NRCS practice 336).

1.2b Inoculation, charging, and biochar-based fertilizers

Fresh biochar can temporarily immobilize nutrients; NRCS and USBI therefore treat inoculation/charging as standard practice before agronomic use (NRCS FAQ; USBI Ag Guidelines · guides farm biochar application; CPS 336 inoculate clause: 2022 PDF).

Three modes (full chapter Biochar Inoculation for Fertilizer):

  1. Nutrient charging — soak/impregnate with NPK solutions, urine, digestate, or manufacture BBFs (pre/post-pyrolysis). meta-analyzes biochar fertilizers meta: BBFs +10% productivity vs fertilized controls (DOI). Scoping review of enrichment methods: Ndoung 2021.
  2. Microbial inoculation — compost tea, PGP/AMF/EM on char carriers; meta shows gains vs biochar alone but weak commercial-field consistency (DOI · meta-analyzes microbial inocula). Habitat in raw field-aged char can be sparse (studies charosphere microbial habitat · DOI).
  3. Co-composting (COMBI) — organic coating + nitrate capture; shows co-compost nitrate capture untreated BC ~60% of control biomass vs co-composted up to ~305% of control (DOI); coating chemistry (Hagemann 2017); COMBI review (reviews co-composted biochar soil effects · DOI).

Practice ranges (sourced): charge 1–3 weeks (USBI); co-compost roughly 5–15% biochar w/w early (Pacific white paper · explains biochar plus compost practice; Antonangelo); enriched fertilizers can work at ≪2 t ha⁻¹ root-zone (liquid nutrient enrichment trials · DOI; urine protocol urine-enhanced biochar field trial · DOI).

Credit/MRV: Puro soil amendment pure/mixed (AF1–AF2) remains eligible end-use; co-composting does not erase aromatic C stability for permanence chemistry (Hagemann 2017; Puro Biochar Methodology Edition 2025). Details: Biochar Inoculation for Fertilizer §9.

1.2c Nutrients & fertilizer chemistry (chapter pointer)

Deep chemistry of N/P/K, CEC/AEC, liming, BBF formulations, and fertilizer-use efficiency: Biochar Nutrients and Fertilizer Chemistry. Headline metas: soil inorganic N mean ~−11% NH₄⁺ / −10% NO₃⁻ in the first year class of evidence (Nguyen 2017); BBFs at mean 0.9 t ha⁻¹ give +10% productivity vs fertilized controls (Melo 2022); biochar+fertilizer field synergy ~+15% (meta-analyzes yields with fertilizer controls · PDF).

1.2d Microbes & soil biology (chapter pointer)

Habitat vs sparse colonization, inocula efficacy, AMF/PGPR, priming, and disease-suppression uncertainty: Biochar Microbes and Soil Biology. Key contrast: biota review (Lehmann 2011) vs sparse field colonization of wood char (Quilliam 2013); inocula meta favors local/fungal over commercial products (DOI).

1.3 Microbial habitat and SOC interactions

Porous carbon provides habitat and alters redox / sorption environments that shape N cycling (see GHG Fluxes). For native soil organic carbon:

  • meta-analyzes stability and priming meta-analysis finds a slight, non-significant mean slowdown of SOC mineralization (−3.8%, 95% CI −8.1% to +0.8%), with significant negative priming in studies under six months (−8.6%) and positive priming in sandy soils (+20.8%) (DOI).
  • Weng et al. (2017) document decade-scale negative priming and SOC accrual under biochar in a field context (DOI).

NRCS positions biochar as long-lived SOM support versus compost’s shorter labile pulse: FAQ cites research that roughly 60–80% of biochar C can remain after 100 years versus 2–14% for compost C, and that ~10 yd³ biochar can match 1-year soil-C increase of ~40 yd³ compost in their comparison framing (FAQ). Cross-check permanence math with Permanence and models soil GHG from biochar (DOI).

3. GHG fluxes from agricultural soils

Deep dive: GHG Fluxes · Nitrous Oxide Fluxes · Methane Fluxes · Priming and SOC Interactions. For farm planning:

  • N₂O: meta-analyzes N2O mitigation classic meta ~−54% across lab and field studies (DOI); Jia et al. (2023) ~−26.8% across incubation, pot and field studies (DOI). H:C_org of the char is a key moderator (Cayuela 2015).
  • CH₄: Mixed; Jia 2023 global mean −13.0%; paddies and flooded systems need site-specific reading (DOI). Woolf et al. (2021) inventory method assumes no net CH₄ change for simplicity (DOI).
  • Inventory conservatism: Woolf 2021 optional N₂O credit only for applications >10 Mg C ha⁻¹ in year 1 (illustrative −23%), and not for organic/forest soils (DOI).

CDR credit tonnage is dominated by stable biochar C, not annual N₂O co-benefits—see Biochar as Durable CDR, MRV Stack.

4. Application methods (pointer)

Full practice notes: Application Rates and Methods · Field Rates by System · Incorporation Topdress Banding · Equipment and Timing · On-farm Trial Design; blends: Biochar Compost and Manure Blends · Co-composting Science · Farm Co-composting Practice.

  • Research rates: often 5–50+ t ha⁻¹ (extremes to 100 t ha⁻¹ in Jeffery 2011) — not default farm advice (DOI).
  • Enriched fertilizers: agronomic doses can be <2 t ha⁻¹ when nutrient-loaded (Schmidt/Pandit line; DOI).
  • NRCS CPS 336 planning: FAQ — 4–10 cubic yards per acre is a good starting range; FY2024 100% biochar payment scenario assumes 4 yd³/ac; blend scenarios specify compost + biochar quantities (FAQ).
  • Incorporation: tillage, trenching, no-till drill/prills, manure injectors, fertigation of fines, permanent-bed mixing; surface-only on slopes risks wind/water loss (FAQ).
  • Pasture: CPS 336 allowed on pasture; not on native rangeland except facilitating Range Planting (550) or Critical Area Planting (342) (FAQ).

8. How soil use connects to permanence and credits

  1. Produce & test to low H:C_org → Properties, Permanence.
  2. Apply to eligible sink (soil / compost / etc.) with chain-of-custody → MRV Stack.
  3. Choose methodology (soil modules differ): Verra VM0044, Puro Biochar Methodology Edition 2025, Isometric Biochar Protocol (soil storage module: https://registry.isometric.com/module/biochar-storage-soil-environments/1.3), CSI Global Biochar C-Sink.
  4. Separate agronomic value (water, nutrients, yield) from credit value (durable C) in farm budgets → Agronomic Economics, Market Volumes and Pricing.

9. Decision tree (evidence-based)

Use this as a planning screen—not a substitute for soil tests or NRCS planners.

  1. Soil screen — Prefer candidates with low OM, coarse texture, or acidic pH (Jeffery 2011/2017; NRCS FAQ). Skip or pilot carefully if alkaline, high-EC, or already high-P with manure-char products (Jeffery 2011; Jeffery 2017; FAQ).
  2. Product screen — Spec sheet: C_org, H:C_org (<0.7 for CPS 336), ash, EC, liming equivalent, metals, PAHs as required by market/standard (Contaminants and Quality; CPS 336 PDF).
  3. Rate screen — Start near NRCS 4–10 yd³/ac unless a vineyard redevelopment or research protocol justifies higher (Oasis 10 t/ac; Dominus 25 t/ac) (FAQ; Oasis; Dominus).
  4. Blend screen — Default to compost/manure charge for agronomy; consider co-composting if a local facility pathway exists (Biochar Compost and Manure Blends).
  5. Placement screen — Band/row for perennials and low doses; broadcast+incorporate for field crops; avoid unprotected surface on HEL slopes (FAQ).
  6. Finance screen — Model yield-only, yield+EQIP, and yield+CDR cases separately (Agronomic Economics; Galinato 2011).
  7. MRV screen — If selling removals: registry choice, application evidence, ownership, no double claim with CPS 336 (MRV Stack, Double Counting and Credit Ownership).

Liming arithmetic (NRCS worked example)

If soil test calls for 1 ton lime/ac and producer applies 1 ton biochar/ac with liming equivalency 9% CaCO₃, remaining lime need ≈ 0.91 ton/ac (FAQ). Using biochar only as a lime substitute does not meet CPS 336’s stated purposes if that is the sole intent—the FAQ warns against treating liming-as-purpose as 336-eligible framing.

CEC / nutrient sponge

Biochar’s surface functional groups and porosity can increase effective nutrient retention, but fresh high-temperature wood chars may have relatively low CEC until oxidized/aged or compost-charged. Operational guidance therefore emphasizes inoculation (CPS 336 PDF). Lehmann et al. 2021 synthesize nutrient and liming co-benefits in the CDR context (DOI).

14. Glossary (soil/ag)

  • AWC — available water capacity (plant-available water between field capacity and permanent wilting point).
  • Charging / inoculation — loading biochar with nutrients and/or microbes (compost, manure, teas, fertilizers) before or during application.
  • CPS 336 — NRCS Conservation Practice Standard Soil Carbon Amendment.
  • H:C_org — molar hydrogen to organic carbon ratio; lower ⇒ more aromatic / persistent; NRCS max 0.7.
  • Liming equivalency — % CaCO₃ neutralizing value of the amendment.
  • Priming — change in mineralization rate of native SOC after amendment (positive = faster loss; negative = slower).
  • yd³/ac — cubic yards per acre (NRCS contracting unit); convert with measured bulk density.
