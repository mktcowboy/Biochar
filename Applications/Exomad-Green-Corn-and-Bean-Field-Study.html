Exomad Green Corn and Bean Field Study

Study note on “Biochar Field Study — Corn and Beans in Controlled Plots”, a 13-page field report published by Exomad Green and run with CIAT (Centro de Investigación Agrícola Tropical) at three sites in Santa Cruz, Bolivia. Biochar was applied once at 4 t ha⁻¹ before a winter bean crop (2024); corn followed with no re-application (PDF). Exomad accounts for roughly 60% of biochar CDR tonnes sold since 2022 (CDR.fyi Snapshot 2025); this is one of the field-study PDFs on its R&D page. Report context: 2026-09-14.

Related notes: Crop Yield Effects · Real World Soil Projects · Soil and Agriculture · Application Rates and Methods · Biochar Inoculation for Fertilizer · Biochar Microbes and Soil Biology · Supply Landscape · Applications Hub · Key Sources

Bottom line

  • Headline gains (as reported): biochar alone +15% corn and +13% beans; biochar + NPKS + microorganisms +32% corn and +25% beans vs an untreated control (PDF p.8; p.10). Direction fits meta-analyses of low-fertility tropical soils (Jeffery 2017 · DOI).
  • Yield differences are not statistically significant. The report’s own ANOVA gives yield p = 0.4481 (beans) and p = 0.924 (corn). Only bean plant height (p < 0.0001) and corn 100-grain weight (p = 0.014) differ significantly (same pages).
  • Only T2 vs T1 isolates biochar. There is no fertilizer-only arm, so the larger T3/T4 gains cannot be credited to biochar.
  • Bean percentages are understated. Against the control, beans gain +14.9% / +24.9% / +34.2% (T2/T3/T4), not the printed 12.94 / 19.90 / 25.47% — the report divided by treatment yield instead of control yield (§6.1).
  • Soil-health conclusions come without soil data. Better nutrient availability, porosity, moisture retention, leaching, CEC, and pH are asserted (PDF p.13), but no post-trial soil tests are shown.
  • Use it as supportive company case evidence with these caveats attached — not as proof of a yield response.

Missing arms: fertilizer only, microbes only, and fertilizer + microbes without biochar. For contrast, Ye et al. (2020) measure the ~+15% biochar effect against fertilizer alone (PDF) — the comparison this design cannot make.

No yield difference is backed by a significant ANOVA in either crop (§6.1–6.2). In corn, 100 kg N ha⁻¹ added almost nothing on top of biochar (T3 vs T2 +0.4%). That would fit water rather than nutrients limiting a drought season, but the report gives no rainfall or soil-moisture data to check.

8. Data-quality issues

  1. Non-significant yields (beans p = 0.4481; corn p = 0.924), with no mean-separation tests; replication, plot size, and statistical model undescribed (PDF p.8; p.10).
  2. Confounded treatments — no fertilizer-only or microbes-only arms (§2).
  3. Mixed percentage bases — beans ÷ treatment, corn ÷ control (§6.1). Press coverage repeated the understated bean figures (Bioenergy International; Carbon Herald).
  4. Date conflict — corn is printed as planted Nov 2023–Jan 2024, before the June 2024 bean crop it followed. June planting + 110–120 days puts bean harvest around late Sep–mid Oct 2024, so Nov 2024–Jan 2025 is the likely intent — unconfirmed (PDF p.6).
  5. Charts disagree with tables — e.g., corn 100-grain-weight bars read ≈19 / 28 / 37 / 37 g against 24.5 / 27.5 / 29.0 / 30.5 g in the table (PDF p.12 vs p.10); bean flower bars also drift (p.9). Use table values.
  6. Soil-health claims without data — no post-trial pH, CEC, OM, bulk density, or moisture measurements (PDF p.13).
  7. Not reported — per-site results, bean species or variety, corn hybrid, incorporation depth, particle size, inoculant product and dose, weather, yield moisture basis.
  8. Conflict of interest — a producer and credit seller reporting its own trial; CIAT is named as collaborator, but no CIAT authors, sign-off, or raw data appear in the PDF.
